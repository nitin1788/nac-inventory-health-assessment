A pharmacy inventory audit is different from a general retail stock count. Batch numbers, expiry
dates, and Schedule H/H1 handling all add complexity that a generic counting checklist doesn't
account for. This is the practical checklist we use as a starting point on our own [inventory audit](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/inventory-audit)
engagements.

## 1. Prepare before you count

- Freeze incoming and outgoing stock movement for the audit window, or log it precisely if a full
  freeze isn't possible.
- Print or export your current system stock list, organized by shelf/location.
- Assign clear ownership — who counts which section, and who reconciles the results.

## 2. Count with batch and expiry in mind

Unlike a general retail count, every line item should capture:

- Quantity on hand
- Batch number
- Expiry date

This is what makes a pharmacy audit useful for more than just a stock-accuracy number — it feeds
directly into [expiry / near-expiry analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/expiry-near-expiry-analysis)
and FEFO compliance checks at the same time.

## 3. Classify what you find

Once the count is complete, it's worth applying an [ABC analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/abc-analysis)
to see which SKUs actually deserve tighter ongoing control. In most pharmacies, a small share of
SKUs drives most of the inventory value:

![Example bar chart showing SKUs grouped into A, B, and C value bands, with A representing about 20% of SKUs but 70% of value](/blog-images/pharmacy-inventory-audit-checklist/abc-classification-diagram.svg)

The "A" band is where tighter counting cycles, stricter reorder discipline, and closer expiry
monitoring pay off the most.

## 4. Reconcile every discrepancy — don't just adjust the number

For every line where the count doesn't match the system:

1. Recheck the physical count before assuming the system is wrong.
2. Categorize the cause — shrinkage, data entry error, timing difference, or expired write-off.
3. Document the adjustment with its reason, not just the corrected number.

A pattern across multiple discrepancies (e.g. one supplier, one category, one staff shift) is
usually more valuable than any single adjustment — it points to a process fix, not just a one-time
correction. This is the core of what we do in our [inventory reconciliation](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/inventory-reconciliation)
engagements.

## 5. Set a cadence, not a one-time event

An annual audit alone will always let some drift go unnoticed for months. Pair it with periodic
[stock verification](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/stock-verification) — a lighter-weight,
repeatable check your own team can run between full audits.
