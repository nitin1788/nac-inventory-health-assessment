Pharmacy stock management — sometimes called pharmacy inventory management — is easy to describe
and hard to actually do well: know what you have, keep enough of what sells, and catch what's
about to expire before it becomes a write-off. Most of the difficulty isn't the concept, it's
that a pharmacy or medical store's stock carries batch numbers, expiry dates, and tight margins
that a generic "count it and reorder" approach ignores. This guide walks through the practical
pieces that actually make stock management work day to day, not just in theory.

## 1. Start from an accurate baseline

None of the steps below mean anything if you don't actually know what's on your shelves right
now. A [pharmacy inventory audit](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/inventory-audit) — a
proper physical count against your records, batch and expiry included — is the honest starting
point. If you haven't run one recently, everything downstream (reorder levels, expiry tracking,
optimization) is built on a guess rather than a fact.

## 2. Classify your stock so effort matches value

Not every SKU deserves the same attention. Two classification methods do most of the work here,
and they answer different questions:

- **[ABC analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/abc-analysis)** ranks stock by value
  contribution — typically a small share of SKUs drives most of your inventory value, and that
  share is where tighter counting cycles and closer monitoring actually pay off.
- **[FSN analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/fsn-analysis)** classifies stock by
  movement speed — fast, slow, or non-moving — which tells you what needs to stay well-stocked
  and what needs a clearance decision instead.

A high-value SKU can still move slowly, and a cheap one can turn over fast, so these two views
genuinely answer different questions. Used together, they tell you both where your money is and
where your shelf space is being wasted.

## 3. Set reorder points instead of reordering by instinct

Reordering by gut feeling reliably produces one of two outcomes: stockouts on things customers
actually want, or excess stock quietly tying up capital. Calculating real
[reorder points and min-max levels](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/reorder-min-max) —
based on your actual demand and supplier lead times, not habit — turns replenishment into a rule
your team can follow instead of a judgment call every single time. This is the single change that
tends to have the most immediate, visible effect on day-to-day stock management.

## 4. Build expiry monitoring into the routine

Expiry write-offs are one of the most direct, measurable costs in running a pharmacy or medical
store — and one of the most preventable. Pharmacy expiry management means mapping expiry dates
at the batch level and flagging what's approaching its window early enough to act on — return to
the supplier, discount, or reallocate — instead of finding out when it's already unsellable. Pair
this with a [FEFO (First-Expiry-First-Out)](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/fefo-implementation)
rotation habit on the shelf itself, so older-dated batches move before newer ones sit behind them.
See our [expiry / near-expiry analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/expiry-near-expiry-analysis)
service for how we approach this in more depth.

## 5. Don't ignore what's already stopped moving

Stock that has genuinely stopped selling is a different problem from stock that's just slow —
and it's worth telling the two apart. [Slow-moving stock](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/slow-moving-stock-analysis)
is still the early-warning stage, with more recovery options available. Stock that's fully
stalled needs a [dead stock](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/dead-stock-analysis)
conversation instead — supplier returns, discounted clearance, or in some cases a write-off.
Catching the slow stage before it becomes the dead stage is most of the battle.

## 6. Let the numbers set your stock levels, not habit

The end goal of all of the above is pharmacy inventory optimization — carrying enough stock to
serve customers reliably without tying up capital you don't need to. [Stock optimization](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/stock-optimization)
sets target levels for each category based on actual demand, not "what we've always kept on the
shelf." It's the step that turns everything above from separate exercises into one coherent
system.

## Retail pharmacy or medical store — the same practical approach applies

Medical store stock management and retail pharmacy stock management run into largely the same
issues — batch/expiry-sensitive stock, thin margins, and a real cost to getting reorder timing
wrong. The distinction between the two is mostly local naming convention and business
registration, not a different set of problems to solve. Whether you run a
[retail pharmacy](/industries/retail-pharmacy) or a
[medical store](/industries/medical-stores), the classification, reorder, and expiry-monitoring
steps above apply the same way.

## Make it a routine, not a one-time fix

An audit once a year, a reorder-level exercise done once and never revisited — these help, but
they let drift creep back in between events. The steadier approach is a lighter-weight,
periodic [stock verification](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/stock-verification) your
own team can run between full audits, catching small discrepancies before they compound into
bigger ones. Pharmacy stock management works best as an ongoing habit built into how the store
runs, not an occasional project.
