Search for ABC analysis or FSN analysis and you'll mostly find academic papers written for hospital pharmacy research — genuinely useful if you're studying the topic, much less useful if you're a retail pharmacy or medical store owner trying to decide what to actually do differently on your shelves. Both techniques classify your stock, but they answer two different questions, and most of the confusion around them comes from treating them as competing methods instead of complementary ones.

## 1. ABC analysis answers: where is my money?

[ABC analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/abc-analysis) ranks stock by value contribution — how much of your total inventory value each item represents. In most pharmacies, a small share of SKUs drives most of the value:

![Example bar chart showing SKUs grouped into A, B, and C value bands, with A representing about 20% of SKUs but 70% of value](/blog-images/pharmacy-inventory-audit-checklist/abc-classification-diagram.svg)

Figures cited in hospital-pharmacy inventory literature typically put "Class A" items at somewhere around 10-15% of total items but 70% or more of expenditure, with "Class B" and "Class C" making up progressively more items for progressively less value. The exact split varies from study to study and won't match your own store precisely — but the underlying pattern, a small group of items carrying most of your inventory value, holds up almost everywhere. Class A is where tighter counting cycles, stricter reorder discipline, and closer monitoring pay off the most, because that's where the money actually is.

## 2. FSN analysis answers: what's actually moving?

[FSN analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/fsn-analysis) classifies stock by movement speed instead — **F**ast-moving, **S**low-moving, or **N**on-moving. This is a completely different lens from value: FSN tells you what needs to stay well-stocked because customers keep buying it, and what's quietly taking up shelf space without moving.

## 3. Why you need both, not just one

A high-value item can still move slowly, and a cheap item can turn over fast. A single expensive, occasionally-prescribed medicine might be Class A by value but Slow-moving by frequency — you want it available, but not sitting in bulk. A cheap, high-turnover OTC product might be Class C by value but Fast-moving — low individual worth, but constant demand that's easy to under-order if you're only looking at value. Neither classification alone tells the full story; used together, they do.

## 4. Combining them: a simple priority matrix

Cross the two classifications and you get a practical priority order, without needing anything more complicated than a spreadsheet:

| | Fast-moving | Slow-moving | Non-moving |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A (high value)** | Highest priority — never run out, order carefully | Watch closely — high value tied up, order in smaller batches | Investigate immediately — high value sitting dead |
| **B (medium value)** | Keep well-stocked, standard reorder rules | Review periodically | Consider clearance |
| **C (low value)** | Keep stocked, simple reorder rules | Low concern | Low concern — clear when convenient |

The top-left and bottom-right corners of a table like this are where the real decisions live — high-value fast movers deserve your tightest attention, and high-value non-movers deserve an honest conversation about what to do with them (our [dead stock analysis](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/dead-stock-analysis) work exists for exactly that corner).

## 5. You don't need the academic version to get the value

None of this requires the statistical rigor of a hospital-pharmacy research paper. A basic spreadsheet with your sales value and movement frequency by SKU is enough to build both classifications and cross them the way the table above does. The goal isn't a perfect academic model — it's knowing which handful of decisions actually matter, out of the hundreds of SKUs on your shelves.
