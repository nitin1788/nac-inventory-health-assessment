**This is general information based on Google's and Meta's published advertising policies and India's Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 — not legal advice.** Advertising rules change, and a specific campaign or claim can raise questions this article doesn't cover. If you're unsure whether a particular ad is compliant, that's a question for a lawyer, not a marketing consultant.

Most pharmacy owners who look into Google Ads or Meta Ads run into the same wall: policy pages, compliance blog posts, and warnings that all seem to be written for a completely different kind of business — a mail-order online pharmacy shipping prescription drugs across state lines, or a large pharmaceutical manufacturer. If you run a physical store and just want more people to find you, it's genuinely unclear which of those rules, if any, apply to you. This is an attempt to sort that out.

## 1. Google's restrictions are written for online pharmacies, not stores like yours

Google's [Healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031) and its [Prescription drug services policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15598647) both restrict the promotion of "online prescribing, dispensing, and sale of prescription drugs" — language aimed squarely at online pharmacies and telemedicine providers. An online pharmacy needs LegitScript certification, has to register with local drug authorities, and is limited to a specific list of approved countries before it can even apply to advertise.

Neither policy mentions a brick-and-mortar pharmacy or medical store advertising its own storefront, hours, delivery service, or general product categories. That's a genuinely different thing from what these rules were written to police. Google's policy also lists India among the locations where a LegitScript-certified online pharmacy can be approved to promote prescription drug services — which only reinforces that the certification requirement is about the online-dispensing business model, not about being a pharmacy in general.

None of this means "anything goes." It means the LegitScript/online-pharmacy certification process almost certainly isn't the hurdle you need to worry about — the real limits for a local store are the drug-advertising rules in Section 3 below, which apply everywhere.

## 2. Meta Ads draws a similar line, with some sharp edges

Meta's advertising standards are stricter in this category than most, for a clear reason: investigations have found large numbers of ads from accounts posing as pharmacies illegally selling controlled substances. That history is why Meta's automated ad review is aggressive around anything that reads as pharmaceutical.

A few specific things reliably get an ad rejected or a page flagged, regardless of whether you're an online pharmacy or a physical one:

- Naming a specific brand-name or generic prescription drug and promoting its use
- Before/after images tied to a health-related product
- Anything that reads as promoting illegal drugs or unsafe supplements

Like Google, Meta requires online pharmacies specifically to be actively LegitScript-certified — again, a rule aimed at the online-dispensing model, not a store advertising that it exists and where it is.

## 3. The rule that actually applies to you, no matter the platform

This is the part most "how to advertise your pharmacy" content — almost all of it written for a US or UK audience — never mentions, and it's the one that matters most if you're advertising in India.

Under **Rules 74 and 78 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945**, no advertisement for a **Schedule H, H1, or X medicine** is allowed without prior sanction from the Central Government. This isn't a Google or Meta rule you can work around with the right certification — it's Indian law, and it applies to a Facebook post or a search ad exactly the same way it applies to a newspaper advertisement.

Separately, and easy to confuse with the above, the **Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954** prohibits advertising a drug as a cure or treatment for a specific list of diseases named in its own Schedule (including cancer, tuberculosis, diabetes, and epilepsy) and a handful of other specific claims — a related but legally distinct restriction from the Schedule H/H1/X rule above. A first conviction under the DMRA can bring up to six months' imprisonment, a fine, or both, with a repeat conviction bringing up to a year — but that penalty attaches to a DMRA violation specifically. We have not confirmed what penalty applies to a Schedule H/H1/X advertising violation itself, since that sits under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act rather than the DMRA — if this ever becomes a real question for your business, get a straight answer from a lawyer rather than assuming either penalty carries over to the other rule.

There has also been public discussion of tightening drug-advertising rules further, including for high-risk medicines and specifically for digital platforms. We're not treating that as settled here — check the current status before relying on it for anything more than "this is a direction, not a done deal."

In practical terms: whatever you advertise, it should never name or promote a specific Schedule H, H1, or X medicine, or make a claim about treating, curing, or preventing a disease. That's true on a hoarding outside your store and it's true in a Google or Meta ad.

## 4. What you can actually advertise

Once the two distinctions above are clear, the list of what's genuinely fine to advertise is longer than it first appears:

- Your store's location, hours, and contact details
- Delivery, home-delivery, or online-ordering *of your service*, not of specific prescription drugs
- General OTC and wellness product categories
- Health checkups, screenings, or services you offer
- Reviews, community presence, and general trust-building content

## 5. A simple check before you launch anything

Before a campaign goes live, it's worth running every ad through three questions:

1. Does it name a specific prescription drug, or a Schedule H/H1/X medicine by name? If yes, stop.
2. Does it claim to treat, cure, or prevent a disease? If yes, stop.
3. Is it actually about your store, your service, or a general product category? If yes, you're almost certainly on safe ground.

Most pharmacy advertising that gets rejected or flagged fails on the first two questions, not because the advertiser is trying to break a rule, but because nobody told them where the actual line was.
