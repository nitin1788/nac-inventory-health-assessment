**This is general information based on India's Draft E-Pharmacy Rules, 2018, related court proceedings, and published legal commentary — not legal advice.** The regulatory position on online medicine sales in India is genuinely unsettled, as this article explains. If you're considering building anything that sells or dispenses medicines online, get a lawyer's opinion before you build it, not after.

If you search for "pharmacy website design," almost everything you'll find — templates, design guides, agency portfolios — assumes you're building an online store. Product search, a shopping cart, "upload your prescription," a payment gateway. That's a fine thing to build if it's actually what you want. But most pharmacy and medical store owners who want "a website" are looking for something much simpler: a page that tells people who you are, where you are, and what you offer, so they trust you enough to call or walk in. Those are two genuinely different things, and in India, one of them comes with a real, still-unresolved regulatory question attached.

## 1. A website and an online pharmacy are not the same decision

A simple business website — the kind we build under [Website Development](/services/digital-marketing/website-development) — exists to give a prospective customer clear, accurate information: your services, your location, your hours, your contact details, maybe a request-a-callback form. It doesn't sell anything directly, and it doesn't touch prescription drugs at all. This is what most of the templates and design guides floating around the internet aren't built for, which is exactly why they default to e-commerce features you probably don't need.

An **online pharmacy** — a site or app that actually lets someone order and pay for medicines to be dispensed and delivered — is a different business model, with a different regulatory position. That's the distinction this article is about.

## 2. What India's e-pharmacy rules actually say (and don't yet say)

In 2018, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare proposed the **Draft E-Pharmacy Rules, 2018**, which would require anyone conducting e-pharmacy business to register with the **Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO)** via Form 18(AA). As of this writing, these rules have never been notified in the Official Gazette — meaning they're still a draft, not a finalized law, years later.

That doesn't mean online medicine sales operate in a lawless gap. The underlying framework already prohibits selling **tranquilizers, psychotropic drugs, narcotics, and other habit-forming drugs** through any e-pharmacy portal, draft rules or not. And the situation is actively being pushed toward resolution: on 16 November 2023, the Delhi High Court directed the Central Government to frame an actual policy for the online sale of medicines within eight weeks. Years after the original draft, this is still a live, unresolved regulatory question — not a settled one you can assume has been quietly sorted out.

## 3. Why this matters before you build anything

If you commission "a website" and it ends up with online ordering, prescription upload, and payment for medicines bolted on because that's what the template included — you've stepped into exactly the regulatory grey zone above, whether that was the intention or not. That's a meaningfully bigger decision than picking a website design, and it's worth making deliberately rather than by default.

If what you actually want is for a nearby customer to find you on Google, see that you're legitimate and well-run, and call or visit — you don't need any of this. A well-built informational site, paired with strong [Local SEO](/services/digital-marketing/local-seo) and a properly managed [Google Business Profile](/services/digital-marketing/google-business-profile), does that job without touching the e-pharmacy question at all. The advertising-side rules on what you can and can't promote — covered in [what pharmacies can and can't advertise on Google & Meta Ads](/blog/pharmacy-google-meta-ads-india-what-you-can-advertise) — apply either way, but the e-pharmacy licensing question only comes up if you're actually selling and dispensing online.

## 4. A simple way to decide

Ask one question: **does the site need to complete a sale of medicine, or does it need to get someone to contact or visit your store?**

- If it's the second one — which is true for most retail pharmacies and medical stores — build the simpler, informational site. It's faster, cheaper, and has none of the regulatory overhead described above.
- If it's genuinely the first one, treat it as the separate, more serious business decision it is, and get proper legal guidance on CDSCO registration and the current state of the draft rules before you build anything, not after.

Most of the "pharmacy website" content and templates you'll come across don't make this distinction at all — they just assume you want the bigger, more complicated thing. You probably don't.
