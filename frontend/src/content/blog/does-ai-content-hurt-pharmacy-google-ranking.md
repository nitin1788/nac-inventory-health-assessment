If you're considering AI-assisted content for your pharmacy's blog or social posts, "will this get me penalized by Google" is a fair question to ask before you start. The short answer, backed by real data rather than assumption, is no — not for the reason most people worry about.

## 1. What Google actually penalizes

Google has been explicit that it does not penalize content simply for being AI-generated. What it does penalize are specific, named practices under its own spam policies — among them **scaled content abuse** (mass-producing pages purely to game rankings, regardless of whether AI is involved) and **site reputation abuse** (unrelated third-party content riding on a trusted site's authority). Its policies also cover thin, low-value content more generally. Notice that none of these are "written with AI assistance" — they're about intent and quality, not the tool used to produce the words.

## 2. The data backs this up

Ahrefs has tested this directly, more than once. Its most recent pass, published in July 2026, analyzed a million pages ranking in the top 10 across 100,000 search queries and found only a gentle, gradual relationship between AI-content level and ranking position — average AI content ticked up from roughly 27% on the #1-ranked page to roughly 31% on the #10-ranked page, not a cliff. An earlier Ahrefs study reported that the large majority of top-ranking pages contained at least some AI-assisted content, with no meaningful correlation between how much a page used and where it ranked. ([Ahrefs' own write-up](https://ahrefs.com/blog/google-doesnt-punish-ai-content/) has the full detail.) If AI authorship itself were being penalized at any meaningful scale, this is not what either study would look like.

## 3. What actually matters is the same as it's always been

Google evaluates content against **E-E-A-T** — experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness — regardless of how it was drafted. For health-related content specifically, this bar is genuinely higher: general search-quality guidance treats medical and health information as a category where accuracy and credibility matter more than most, not less. A generic, unreviewed piece of content risks failing that bar whether a person or an AI tool wrote the first draft.

## 4. Which is exactly why human review still matters

None of this means AI-assisted content is a shortcut past quality control — it means the actual risk was never "AI wrote this," it's "nobody checked whether this is accurate and genuinely useful before publishing it."
