Most people learn FIFO — First-In-First-Out — as the default rule for rotating stock: sell the oldest stock first. It's a reasonable general-retail rule, and it's wrong often enough for pharmacy stock specifically that following it without thinking can actually cause more expired write-offs, not fewer. FEFO exists because of that gap.

## 1. What FIFO actually assumes

FIFO rotates stock based on **when it arrived** — the oldest received batch gets sold first, on the assumption that "oldest received" and "closest to expiring" are basically the same thing. For most general retail products, that assumption holds up fine.

## 2. Why pharmacy stock breaks that assumption

A pharmacy or medical store doesn't always receive stock in a tidy expiry-date order. A shipment that arrives later can carry an **earlier** expiry date than a batch that's already been sitting on your shelf for weeks — different manufacturing runs, different supplier batches, or promotional stock with a shorter shelf life can all cause this. If you're rotating purely by "what arrived first," that newer, sooner-to-expire batch sits behind older stock and quietly runs down the clock unnoticed, until it's the thing that gets written off.

## 3. What FEFO does differently

**FEFO — First-Expiry-First-Out** — rotates stock by actual expiry date, not by when it arrived. Whichever batch is closest to expiring gets sold first, regardless of which shipment it came from. This is the standard recommended rotation method for pharmacy stock specifically, for the direct reason that expiration dates correlate with medication efficacy and, ultimately, patient safety — not just with avoiding a write-off.

## 4. This isn't a replacement for FIFO, it's a correction for where FIFO fails

Most of the time, FIFO and FEFO agree — older-received stock usually is closer to expiring, so there's no conflict. The point of deliberately using FEFO isn't to throw out the FIFO habit; it's to catch the specific, recurring cases where the two disagree, which is exactly where expiry write-offs quietly happen if nobody's checking.

## 5. Making it work in practice

FEFO only works if expiry dates are actually visible and checked at the point of shelving, not just recorded in a system somewhere. That means batch and expiry data captured during receiving, stock physically arranged so the nearest-expiry batch is easiest to reach first, and a habit of checking dates rather than assuming shelf position tells the whole story.
