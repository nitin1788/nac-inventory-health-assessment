**This is general information based on India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA), 2023 and its published cookie-consent guidance — not legal advice.** Data-protection rules and their enforcement are still evolving; if you need a definitive answer for your specific site or campaign, that's a question for a lawyer, not a marketing consultant.

If your pharmacy's website has Google Analytics installed, or you're running Google Ads or Meta Ads, there's a real Indian law being phased in that will apply before any of that tracking is technically allowed to run — and it's easy to miss, because almost nothing written about "pharmacy analytics" or "conversion tracking" mentions it. Most of it is written for a US audience and talks about HIPAA instead, which doesn't apply here at all. This is what actually does, and when.

## 1. DPDPA is being phased in — know what's coming and when

India's **Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA), 2023** was passed in 2023, and its implementing Rules were notified on 13 November 2025 — but not everything in it is in force yet. The core notice-and-consent provisions (Sections 5 and 6 of the Act) are scheduled to take effect on **13 May 2027**, with the Consent Manager framework arriving a year earlier, from 13 November 2026. Once those provisions commence, the law follows an **opt-in model**: cookies used for analytics, advertising, or tracking aren't supposed to run until a visitor has explicitly agreed to them — a meaningfully different bar than a cookie banner that just informs people tracking is happening.

This will apply to more than just Google Analytics. Google Ads and Meta Ads both drop their own tracking cookies or pixels the moment someone lands on your site from a campaign, and those will be covered too once the relevant provisions are in force. The practical point for right now: the deadline is public and the direction is set, so it's worth getting ready ahead of mid-2027 rather than waiting until it's actually enforceable to start.

## 2. What a compliant cookie notice actually needs to say

A proper cookie consent setup isn't just "Accept" and "Reject" buttons — the underlying policy needs to disclose, in plain language:

- What cookies are being used
- The purpose of each one — functionality, preferences, analytics, or marketing are the usual categories
- Which third parties the data is shared with (Google and Meta, if you're running their tools)
- How long each cookie's data is retained

Most template privacy policies skip most of this, or bundle everything into one vague paragraph. Once DPDPA's notice-and-consent provisions are in force, that won't be enough — the specificity is the point — so it's a reasonable standard to build toward now rather than only once it's mandatory.

## 3. Google Consent Mode is the practical bridge

The good news is that this doesn't mean choosing between compliance and having working analytics. **Google Consent Mode** is built specifically for this situation: it lets Google Analytics and Google Ads adjust what they measure based on the visitor's actual consent choice, instead of either ignoring consent entirely or breaking your tracking the moment someone declines. Setting this up correctly is a genuinely technical step, not just a checkbox in a website builder — it's part of what a proper [Analytics & Conversion Tracking](/services/digital-marketing/analytics-conversion-tracking) setup should include for any Indian site, not an optional extra.

## 4. It's not just cookies — WhatsApp counts too

DPDPA governs personal data generally, not only website cookies. If your pharmacy uses WhatsApp for refill reminders, order updates, or general customer communication, the phone numbers and message history you're holding are personal data under the same law. This is a different, broader rule than HIPAA — the US framework that most "healthcare marketing compliance" content defaults to, and that simply doesn't apply to an Indian business. DPDPA doesn't require a US-style HIPAA compliance program; it requires that you're not collecting or holding more personal data than you have a legitimate reason for, and that people know what you're doing with it.

The advertising restrictions covered in [what pharmacies can and can't advertise on Google & Meta Ads](/blog/pharmacy-google-meta-ads-india-what-you-can-advertise) are about *what you say* in an ad. DPDPA is a separate, additional layer about *what data you collect* while running that ad or tracking who responded to it — the two aren't the same rule, and meeting one doesn't automatically mean you've met the other.

## 5. A simple starting checklist

Ahead of DPDPA's consent provisions taking effect (or now, if you'd rather be early than scrambling later):

1. Confirm your site actually waits for consent before Google Analytics, Google Ads, or Meta Pixel tracking fires — not just displays a banner alongside tracking that's already running.
2. Check that your cookie/privacy policy names the specific cookies, purposes, third parties, and retention periods — not a generic paragraph.
3. If you use Google Ads or Analytics, confirm Google Consent Mode is actually configured, not just assumed.
4. Review what personal data you're storing from WhatsApp or contact-form enquiries, and whether you actually need all of it.

None of this is about avoiding analytics or ads — it's about running them in a way that holds up if anyone ever asks how your site handles a visitor's data.
