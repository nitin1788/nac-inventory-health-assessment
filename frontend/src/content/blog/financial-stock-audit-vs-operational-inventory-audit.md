Search for "pharmacy stock audit" in India and the results are almost entirely Chartered Accountant and financial-audit firms — not operations consultants. That's not a fluke of search results; it reflects a real split in what "stock audit" actually means, depending on who's doing it and why. If you're trying to figure out what kind of audit you actually need, that distinction matters more than which firm you eventually call.

## 1. A financial stock audit is about proving value, to someone else

A financial stock audit — the kind Chartered Accountant and financial-audit firms typically offer — exists to verify the value of your stock for an external party, most often a lender who's financed your inventory or business. It's usually conducted by a Chartered Accountant or another qualified auditor appointed by the company or the lender, and its purpose is statutory or contractual: confirming that the stock backing a loan or a set of accounts is actually there, at the value claimed. Firms working in this space commonly cite Schedule M and Schedule P of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945 as the regulatory basis for the batch, expiry, and cold-chain verification a pharmaceutical stock audit involves — a real, India-specific regulatory grounding worth knowing, even if it's not the reason most pharmacy owners come looking for an audit.

## 2. An operational inventory audit is about fixing what's actually wrong

An [operational inventory audit](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/inventory-audit) starts from a different question entirely: not "can we confirm this value for someone external," but "why doesn't our system match what's actually on the shelf, and what do we do about it." When a physical count doesn't reconcile with your records, a financial audit typically stops at documenting the discrepancy. An operational audit goes further — is it one supplier consistently short-shipping, one staff shift where errors cluster, a specific category with a process gap — because that pattern, once found, is what actually stops the same discrepancy from recurring next month. This is the core of what we do in [inventory reconciliation](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/inventory-reconciliation) and ongoing [stock verification](/services/inventory-operations-consulting/stock-verification) work.

## 3. How to tell which one you actually need

- A bank, lender, or your own statutory accounts require a certified stock valuation → that's a **financial stock audit**, and it needs a Chartered Accountant or equivalent qualified auditor, not an operations consultant.
- Stock keeps not matching your records, shrinkage feels higher than it should, or you simply don't trust your current numbers → that's an **operational inventory audit**, focused on finding and fixing the process cause, not certifying a value.

## 4. They're not competing, and sometimes both matter

A financial audit that turns up a discrepancy tells you *that* something's wrong; it's not designed to tell you *why*. If a lender-required audit surfaces a gap, an operational audit is often the sensible next step — not to replace the financial one, but to actually get underneath the number and stop it from happening again.
